# Perfect Competition: Engagement Brief

## The problem

The farm must determine how to allocate its 64 available planting beds among tomatoes, carrots, and mesclun during a 36-week growing season to maximize seasonal profit. The maximum allocations are 20 tomato beds, 20 carrot beds, and 30 mesclun beds. These crop limits total 70 beds, but the farm only has 64 available, so it cannot plant the maximum amount of every crop.

Each crop generates different revenue and requires different amounts of labor. The farm must also account for fertilizer expenses, available labor, temporary worker costs, diminishing returns, and $20,000 in fixed costs. The challenge is determining which additional beds generate enough revenue to justify the additional costs and labor required.

## What I am assuming

I am assuming that the crop with the highest revenue per bed will not necessarily generate the highest overall profit because labor requirements and diminishing returns affect the cost of adding more beds. Tomatoes generate $8,800 per bed but have a 10% diminishing-returns rate. Carrots generate $2,094 per bed with a 2.5% rate, while mesclun generates $2,700 per bed with a 1.25% rate.

I also assume that leaving some beds unplanted could be more profitable than using all available land if the additional revenue from another bed is less than the additional labor and fertilizer costs required to produce it.

## Hypothesis

I predict that allocating 18 beds to tomatoes, 18 beds to carrots, and 25 beds to mesclun will provide a profitable balance between revenue and operating costs. This combination uses 61 of the 64 available beds, leaving three unplanted.

I would allocate 18 beds to tomatoes because their $8,800 revenue per bed is significantly higher than carrots at $2,094 and mesclun at $2,700. However, tomatoes also have the highest diminishing-returns rate at 10%, so I expect their increasing labor requirements to make the final tomato beds less attractive as production expands.

I would allocate 18 beds to carrots because their 2.5% diminishing-returns rate should cause labor requirements to increase more slowly. I would allocate 25 beds to mesclun because it generates more revenue per bed than carrots while having the lowest diminishing-returns rate at 1.25%. Based on these differences, I expect the 18/18/25 allocation to balance the higher revenue potential of tomatoes with the slower-growing labor requirements of carrots and mesclun.

## How I would know I was wrong

I would know my hypothesis was incorrect if the Excel model identifies a different feasible planting combination that generates greater seasonal profit. For example, the model might show that planting all 20 tomato beds is profitable despite their increasing labor requirements, or that allocating more beds to mesclun and fewer to carrots would improve overall profit.

I will compare my predicted allocation with the model's results and examine how revenue, labor costs, available labor, and diminishing returns affect the optimal planting decision. This comparison will help determine which factors I underestimated or overestimated in my initial prediction.
